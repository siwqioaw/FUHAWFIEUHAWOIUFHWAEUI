## In one line
A storyteller explains a world where you only see colour after you touch your soulmate, and shows it on a couple of
strangers in 5 s. Then the story: Liam and Sarah are still grey after five years and marry anyway. At the wedding Liam
trips, his best friend catches his hand, the world bursts into colour, and the friend says "Your tie is red."

## Why it works
1. **The rule is said and shown in 5.3 s, on strangers** (0.00-5.32). The picture drains to grey on "can't" (0.72-0.80),
   a girl appears on "until you touch" (1.53), their hands meet on "them" (3.38), and a white flash opens on a vivid
   world on "your vision bursts" (3.60-3.68). The viewer learns the visual language before the story needs it, so the
   twist at 28.1 needs no explanation. *seen + heard + measured*
2. **Colour is the plot.** The story is shot in normal colour. Only five "seeing" shots (centred back views, the
   character looking into the world) change the grade: grey for "still saw the world in black and white" (8.6), dull
   for the failed wedding hug (18.9), vivid for the twist (28.1). The twist shot copies the failure shot's framing
   (back view, the same flower arch) with the opposite grade, and Liam is now alone in it. *measured + seen*
3. **A false ending, then a twist that was planted.** Sarah's teary acceptance and the beaming walk down the aisle
   (21.0-24.2) sound like a bittersweet end. A stumble (24.2) forces a touch from someone else. The best friend was in
   the front row of the wedding wide (13.52-13.85). *seen + heard*
4. **The punchline uses the rule.** "Your tie is red." (33.42) is the only line of dialogue. Naming a colour proves the
   friend sees colour too, and his smug grin (33.45) plus the bride's unimpressed face behind Liam (31.2-32.3) turn the
   romance into a joke. The red tie has been on screen since 13.5. *seen + heard*
5. **The sound follows the grade:** sub-bass thuds on every drain (0.68, 8.6-9.3, 18.7), bright shimmers on both bursts
   (3.45-4.55, 28.15-28.75), and the loudest moment of the video, a 1.1 s boom at -6 to -10 dBFS RMS, starts on the
   tail of "instantly" (30.30). *measured*
6. **No air.** The narration runs at 4.66 words/s and never stops for more than 0.24 s. Each sentence gets one shot,
   and almost every shot opens on a 0.2-0.3 s camera move. *measured + seen*

## 11. Principles to reuse / things not to copy
**Reuse:**
1. Make the title the rule, and say it as the first line in second person ("In this world, you..."), with the key noun
   in caps.
2. Demonstrate the rule on an anonymous pair inside the first 5 s, showing both states, before the story starts. The
   twist then needs no explaining.
3. Make the rule visible with one global, repeatable change to the picture (here, saturation), used only on "seeing"
   shots: a centred back view into a vanishing point that starts close and pulls back. Keep the rest of the story in
   normal colour, so the audience sees what the characters can't.
4. Give each state its own entrance. Drain: a desaturation ramp inside the shot (5-18 frames, to about 25% of normal
   chroma), a sub thud, optionally a slice-glitch cut. Burst: a warm white light-leak flash with 2-3 near-white frames
   hiding the cut, a saturation bloom over 0.1-0.25 s (about 2x normal, with a bluer sky), a shimmer.
5. Rhyme the failure shot with the twist shot: same framing and place, opposite state, and change who is in it.
6. A story spine for "in this world" twists: rule demo, couple and obstacle, defiance, a test full of hope, failure,
   acceptance as a false ending, an accident, the rule firing on someone unexpected (planted earlier in a wide),
   reaction shots, one line of dialogue that proves the twist through the rule.
7. Echo the rule's wording at the twist ("bursts into vibrant colors" / "exploded into vibrant color").
8. Put a prop the rule makes meaningful on screen early (the red tie, from 13.5) and pay it off in the last line.
9. One sentence per shot, cutting on its first word. Open every shot with a 0.2-0.3 s eased push-in or pull-back of
   the Roblox camera, then hold.
10. One face per character per shot, chosen for that sentence's emotion (tears and blush for the romance beats). Swap
    mid-shot only when the swap is the action.
11. Continuous narration at about 4.5-4.8 words/s; let the hits sit under or between words rather than waiting for
    pauses. Make the biggest boom land right after the twist line.
12. Captions of 1-3 words, high in frame (block centred at about 25% from the top), white with a black outline, a
    Montserrat-like semibold, lower case with caps on the stressed words, no emoji; frame faces just below them.
13. Leave background gags for rewatches (a cat guest, a smiley-head guest, the bride's face).
14. End on the punchline and a boom with about 0.3 s of tail and no outro.

**Don't copy:** this premise (colour only after touching your soulmate is itself a well-known "soulmate AU" fan-fiction
trope; background knowledge, not from the video), the best-friend-at-the-wedding twist, the line "Your tie is red",
the names Liam and Sarah, the avatars (the alien-cat tee, the duck tee, the blond waistcoat friend), the wedding venue
build. The caption template and the edit transitions are common editor styles, not this creator's own.

**Against 001's lessons:**
- Inflated cut list: holds (35 raw, 23 real). The causes here also include an edit spin-blur, white flashes and a
  caption swap. Missed swoops: none here.
- Faces swapped as textures, no lip sync: holds. But the swaps come at cuts (about one face per shot), not mid-shot on
  the word: about 25 changes, only one mid-shot.
- Captions: same look (white, black outline, Montserrat-like, selective caps), but high in frame (25% from the top
  against 79%), bigger, 1-3 words against a clause, and no emoji.
- Bass hits in gaps after punchlines: the hits are there, but with no gaps they sit under the narration and track the
  grade; the onsets-outside-words list misses the two big booms.
- Different here: the edit carries the concept (spin-blur, slice-glitch, light-leak flashes, grade ramps), where 001's
  energy came only from the camera and animation in Roblox; the narrator is an off-screen storyteller, not an on-screen
  character; plain storybook register, not slang; it doesn't loop by its words.
